A07 - Bracket FEA

Objective

The objective is to use finite element analysis to improve the bracket from A05 and A06 so it is stiffer, stronger, and lighter. The workflow has an outer loop and an inner loop. The inner loop validates each result: set up a static stress study with constraints, loading, and a mesh, solve, read the maximum stress, maximum displacement, and safety factor, then refine the mesh until the results agree within 7% (or stop after the third attempt and keep the finest mesh). The outer loop uses the validated results to change the geometry, adding material where the part is weak and removing it where it carries little load, and it runs at least three times. The material stays Aluminum 6061 throughout.

Analyze

Material and Load Case

Aluminum 6061, unchanged from A05 and A06 (6061-T6 (SS) in the SolidWorks library: E = 10,007,604 psi, Sy = 39,885 psi, density 0.0975 lb/in3).

Load. 1,600 lbf total, applied downward on the cylinder's lower surface. This is the same W = 2F from A05, where each side of the strap carries F = 800 lbf.

Constraint. Fixed geometry on the interior faces of the T-slot, which is where the bracket hangs from the T-beam.

Study type. Static stress analysis with a solid, blended curvature-based mesh. For each design I solved with a default (medium) mesh, then with a finer mesh, and compared the two.

Results

The safety factor is computed from the maximum von Mises stress criterion, so it is the yield strength divided by the local von Mises stress. The minimum value at the bottom of each factor of safety legend is the safety factor reported in the tables, and a value below 1 means the material yields under the load. The von Mises plots show where that happens.

Design II (Outer Loop 2)

Geometry

Design I fails by a wide margin, with a safety factor of 0.18 against a target of 4. Almost all of the stress sits in the connecting plate (Feature B), while the block around the T-slot stays nearly unstressed. For Design II I sketched the whole back profile of the bracket and extruded it 0.2 in, which thickened the plate, and I added 0.1 in fillets at the A-B and B-C junctions to reduce the stress concentration where the plate meets the cylinder and the block.

Design Decisions

Design I to Design II. The A05 hand calculations treated the plate as an axial bar. In the model, the cylinder hangs to one side of the plate, so the 1,600 lbf load acts offset from the plate and bends it, which the axial model did not include. The 0.17 in thick plate carried that bending and deflected about 0.46 in at the cylinder. Thickening the plate was the change that mattered: stress fell 64% and displacement fell 87%, for 24% more volume.

Design II to Design III. The block was nearly unstressed in every plot, so Design III removed its corners, which took the volume back to 4.74 in3, and enlarged the A-B fillet to 0.25 in. Together the two changes cost no stiffness (displacement 3.5% lower), but the larger fillet did not lower the peak stress: it went up 13%, and the peak sits on the plate face above the cylinder, not at the junction. The stress there is set by bending of the plate, and neither change touched that.

Final design: Design III. The objective asks for a part that is stiff, strong, and light. Design III has the lowest deflection of the three (0.05612 in, 88% lower than Design I) and the lowest mass (0.46 lb, equal to Design I), and its safety factor of 0.450 is within 11% of the strongest design, Design II (0.507). Per pound of material it is also the strongest: a safety factor of about 0.98 per lb against 0.87 for Design II. Design II wins on strength alone, but it costs 26% more mass for that. None of the three reaches a safety factor of 1, let alone the target of 4 from A05, so the bracket is not safe at 1,600 lbf yet. Getting from 88,720 psi to the allowable 9,971 psi (Sy / 4) needs about 8.9 times less stress. If plate bending governs, bending stress falls with the square of thickness, so that points to a plate about 3 times thicker, or to moving the load path so the cylinder is centered on the plate and the bending moment disappears. Either would be the next outer loop.

Mistakes

  • Mass reported with no material assigned. The first Mass Properties reading for Design I gave 0.17 lb at a density of 0.04 lb/in3. 6061-T6 is 0.0975 lb/in3, so the part had no material assigned in SolidWorks and fell back to the default density of 1 g/cm3. The study had the correct material, so the stress, displacement, and safety factor results were not affected, but the mass was wrong, and comparing it with Design II (0.58 lb, correct density) would have shown a mass increase of 3.4 times instead of 26%. I caught it from the density line, assigned 6061-T6 to the part, and re-read the mass as 0.46 lb.
  • Could not find the element count or the factor of safety plot at first. The element count is not in the Mesh menu; it is under right-click Mesh, Details. The factor of safety plot is not created by default; it comes from right-click Results, Define Factor of Safety Plot.
  • Material added where it was not needed. The Design II back-profile extrude covered the block as well as the plate, so about 1.16 in3 of new material went in, part of it into the region that carries almost no stress. Design III then removed block material to recover the mass.

Reflections

Time spent: 5.5 hours of work in total, from reading the assignment on Oct 21 to finishing the page on Oct 22: 4 hours for the design and the studies in SolidWorks, and 1.5 hours writing the portfolio page.

Engineering lessons:

  • FEA checked an assumption that the hand calculations could not. In A05 each feature was sized in isolation, with the plate modeled as an axial bar. The 3D model showed the load path is offset, so the plate also bends, and the safety factor came out at 0.185 where the hand calculation promised 4. The error was in the model of the load path, not in the arithmetic.
  • Refining the mesh once was enough for every design. Going from roughly 9,000-11,000 elements to 18,000-21,000 elements changed the stress by 0.6% to 3.8%, the displacement by 0.1% to 1.0%, and the safety factor by 0.7% to 4.0%, all inside the 7% limit. Displacement moved the least in Designs II and III (0.09%), which fits the fact that it depends on the stiffness of the whole part, while stress depends on the local mesh at the hot spot. Every mesh took 1 to 3 seconds to build, so on a part this small the cost of a finer mesh was negligible. I expect that to change for larger parts, where element count drives solve time.
  • A linear static study has a range where it is valid. Design I deflected 0.46 in and SolidWorks flagged excessive displacement. At that point the 216,000 psi is an indication of where and how badly the plate is overstressed, not a number to design to. After the plate was thickened the deflection fell to 0.058 in, an 87% drop.
  • A fillet only helps if the peak sits at the corner. Raising the A-B fillet from 0.1 in to 0.25 in did not lower the peak stress, because the peak was on the plate face above the cylinder, where bending governs. The stress plot, not the shape of the part, shows where to put the material.
  • Mass has to be checked from the part, not the study. The simulation material and the part material are separate settings in SolidWorks. The study ran correctly with 6061-T6 while the part had no material, which gave a mass off by a factor of 2.7.
  • Strength, stiffness, and mass pulled against each other. Design II bought a 64% stress drop for 26% more mass, and Design III gave the mass back at the cost of a 13% stress increase. None of the three met the safety factor, which shows the limit comes from the load path and the plate, not from the amount of material in the block.

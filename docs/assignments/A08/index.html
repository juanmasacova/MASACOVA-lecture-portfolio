A8 – Gears

Objective

The objective is to design a gearbox that can be assembled and disassembled around a pair of meshed spur gears: size the input and output shafts for strength, design and select the journal bearings that carry the shafts, sketch a two-component housing and decide how its halves fasten together, and build the result as a parametric CAD model with the McMaster-Carr parts downloaded into it.

Given Data and Assumptions

Spur gear ratio 24:9, with the pinion (7880K19) on the input shaft and the gear (7880K24) on the output shaft. The output gear torque and speed are 1.25 in·lbf and 1100 rpm. The input (pinion, power-transmission) shaft is 0.125 in in diameter and the output (gear, short) shaft is 0.25 in in diameter, both 1 in long. Equations for the shafts come from Machinery's Handbook (empirical units) and the lecture.

  • The shafts are strong enough to be checked with the Machinery's Handbook equations.
  • The gear train is ideal (no losses).
  • The moment of the radial force from the gear train is small enough to ignore, so the shafts carry torsion only.
  • The shafts are press fit through the hubs of the gears.
  • There is enough clearance between the gears and the gearbox.
  • The shaft holes use a running sliding fit.

1. Design of the Shafts

Gear Train Setup: Speeds, Torques, and Power

Both shaft checks and the bearing loads need the speed, torque, and power of each shaft, so I worked these out first. The pinion is the small gear, so it turns faster than the gear and carries less torque.

  • Input speed: N1/N2 = 24/9, so N1 = 1100(24/9) = 2933.3 rpm.
  • Input torque: for an ideal train, power in equals power out, so T1N1 = T2N2 and T1 = 1.25(9/24) = 0.46875 in·lbf.
  • Power: P = Tω = T(2πN/60) = 1.25(2π(1100)/60) = 143.99 in·lbf/s. With 1 hp = 6600 in·lbf/s this is 0.021817 hp, the same on both shafts. The Handbook equations use horsepower, so P = TN/63025 with T in in·lbf and N in rpm.

Gear train setup: N1 = 2933.3 rpm, T1 = 0.46875 in·lbf, P = 0.021817 hp.

1a. Input Shaft (Power-Transmission), D = 0.125 in

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: T1 = 0.46875 in·lbf, N1 = 2933.3 rpm, P = 0.021817 hp, D1 = 0.125 in, L = 1 in. Unknowns: shear stress τ, allowable power Pallow, minimum diameter Dmin, allowable stress τallow.

ii. Free body diagram. Three sketches. The whole shaft has the drive torque T1 at the input end and the equal and opposite reaction at the pinion, where the mesh pushes back. Cutting the shaft anywhere along its length and summing moments about the axis gives an internal torque T = T1, constant along the shaft, with no shear force or bending moment because the radial force is ignored. On the cross-section the shear stress is zero at the center and reaches its maximum at the surface (c = D/2 = 0.0625 in), growing linearly with radius.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Torsion: τ = Tc/J with c = D/2 and J = πD4/32, so τ = 16T/(πD3).
  • Machinery's Handbook Eq. 4a for a main power-transmitting shaft: Pallow = D3N/80, and solved for the diameter (Eq. 4b) Dmin = ∛(80P/N).
  • To connect Eq. 4a to stress, set P = Pallow and use T = 63025P/N. This gives T = 63025D3/80, so τallow = 16(63025)/(80π). D cancels, which shows that Eq. 4a is built on a fixed allowable shear stress.
  • The shaft is sufficient if P ≤ Pallow, D ≥ Dmin, and τ ≤ τallow.

iv. Numerical solution.

1b. Output Shaft (Short Shaft), D = 0.25 in

Same method, with the Handbook equations for small, short shafts and the output speed N2 = 1100 rpm.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: T2 = 1.25 in·lbf, N2 = 1100 rpm, P = 0.021817 hp, D2 = 0.25 in, L = 1 in. Unknowns: τ, Pallow, Dmin, τallow.

ii. Free body diagram. The gear, press fit on the shaft, is at one end. The mesh applies T2 at the gear, and the equal and opposite output load acts at the other end. The cut gives T = T2 with no shear or bending, and the cross-section has τ = 0 at the center and τmax at c = D/2 = 0.125 in.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • τ = 16T/(πD3), as before.
  • Handbook Eq. 6a for small, short shafts: Pallow = D3N/38, and Dmin = ∛(38P/N) (Eq. 6b).
  • With T = 63025P/N at P = Pallow: τallow = 16(63025)/(38π), and D again cancels.

iv. Numerical solution.

2. Journal Bearings

2a. Size, Pressure, Velocity, and PV

The bearing loads come from the gear forces, so I needed the real gear geometry from the McMaster-Carr drawings.

McMaster-Carr drawing of the pinion, 7880K19.

McMaster-Carr drawing of the gear, 7880K24.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: T1 = 0.46875 in·lbf, T2 = 1.25 in·lbf, N1 = 2933.3 rpm, N2 = 1100 rpm, d1 = 0.375 in, d2 = 1 in, α = 20°, D1 = 0.125 in, D2 = 0.25 in, and the application limits table. Unknowns: Ft, Fr, bearing load Fb, bearing length Lb, pressure P, velocity V, PV.

Bearing size. The bearing inside diameter equals the shaft diameter (0.125 in for the pinion shaft, 0.25 in for the gear shaft), and each shaft has two bearings, one at each end, with the gear between them, so by symmetry each bearing carries half the load. I chose a bearing length Lb = 0.25 in. It has to fit with the gear on the 1 in shaft: pinion 0.315 + 2(0.25) = 0.815 in and gear 0.375 + 2(0.25) = 0.875 in, both under 1 in.

ii. Free body diagram. End view: the shaft pushes down on the bearing bore with Fb, the pressure acts on the loaded half of the bore, and the housing reacts with an equal and opposite force R = Fb. Side view: the bearing length Lb and the shaft diameter D define the projected area A = D·Lb.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Tangential force (lecture): Ft = 2T/d. Computed from the pinion and from the gear, it must agree.
  • Radial force (lecture): Fr = Ft tan α.
  • Load per bearing: Fb = Fr/2.
  • Pressure on the projected area: P = Fb/(D·Lb).
  • Surface speed: V = πDN/12 in ft/min, with D in inches and N in rpm.
  • PV = P·V, in psi·ft/min.

iv. Numerical solution. Ft = 2(0.46875)/0.375 = 2(1.25)/1 = 2.500 lbf from both gears, so the two agree. Fr = 2.5 tan 20° = 0.910 lbf, and Fb = 0.910/2 = 0.455 lbf. The resultant tooth force W = Ft/cos α = 2.66 lbf is shown on the scan for reference; the sizing follows the lecture and uses the radial force.

2b. Thickness of the Journal Bearing

I modeled the bearing as a thin-walled sleeve loaded by the bearing pressure on its bore. The allowable stress is the porous bronze load capacity from Table 6, σallow = 4500 psi, and the loading is the pressure from 2a.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: P1 = 14.6 psi, P2 = 7.28 psi, D1 = 0.125 in, D2 = 0.25 in, Lb = 0.25 in, σallow = 4500 psi. Unknowns: hoop stress σh and minimum wall thickness tmin.

ii. Free body diagram. The ring is cut along a diameter and the top half is the free body. The bore pressure pushes the half up with a resultant FP = P(2r)Lb, acting over the projected width 2r. Each of the two cut faces carries the hoop stress force σh·t·Lb downward, where t Lb is the area of the cut.

iii. Model and algebraic solution. ΣFy = 0 gives 2σh·t·Lb − P(2r)Lb = 0. Lb cancels, so σh = Pr/t. Setting σh = σallow and solving for the thickness gives tmin = Pr/σallow.

iv. Numerical solution.

Pinion bearing (r = 0.0625 in)Gear bearing (r = 0.125 in)
FP = P(2r)Lb14.6(2(0.0625))(0.25) = 0.456 lbf7.28(2(0.125))(0.25) = 0.455 lbf
Force per cut face = FP/20.228 lbf0.2275 lbf
tmin = Pr/σallow14.6(0.0625)/4500 = 2.03 × 10-4 in7.28(0.125)/4500 = 2.02 × 10-4 in

The minimum wall thickness required for strength is about 0.0002 in for both the pinion and gear bearings. The two values are nearly identical because Pr = Fb/(2Lb) is the same for both bearings. A wall this thin cannot be manufactured or handled, so the actual thickness is set by standard bearing sizes, and the strength requirement is satisfied by a very large margin.

3. Gearbox Housing

The housing is two square plates that hold the bearings, with the gears in the gap between them. Each plate has a bearing hole for each shaft, sized to its bearing, and the shaft centers are CD = (0.375 + 1.000)/2 = 0.6875 in apart, the center distance of the meshed pair.

How the two components fit together. Plate A is secured to plate B by four #4-40 flat head screws, passing through plate A (countersunk) and a spacer at each corner into tapped holes in plate B. The spacers hold the plates 0.500 in apart, which is the gap for the gears (0.25 in bearing + 0.50 in gap + 0.25 in bearing = the 1 in shaft length). I chose screws into tapped holes so no nuts are needed, the countersunk heads sit flush so both outer faces stay flat, and the screws can be removed and replaced repeatedly, which a snap fit would not allow. Washers on the shafts next to the gears hold the gears in position axially so the teeth stay aligned.

Clearance. The gear has a 1.042 in outside diameter, so its teeth reach 0.865 in from the plate center toward the gear side, 0.135 in short of the plate edge. The gear center is 0.921 in from a corner screw, which leaves 0.275 in between the gear teeth and a 0.25 in diameter spacer (0.921 − 0.521 − 0.125). The hub radii (0.140 in for the pinion and 0.315 in for the gear) sum to 0.455 in, less than the 0.6875 in center distance, so the hubs do not touch.

5. Lessons Learned

  • Speed up means torque down. The pinion turns 2.67 times faster than the gear and carries 0.375 times the torque, and the power is the same on both shafts. Computing the power from both sides was a quick check on the speed and torque conversions.
  • Units decide the formula. The Handbook equations are in horsepower, inches, and rpm, so I had to convert torque and speed to horsepower with 1 hp = 6600 in·lbf/s (P = TN/63025) before using them.
  • The Handbook shaft equations hide a stress limit. Substituting T = 63025P/N into Eq. 4a and Eq. 6a, the diameter cancels and leaves a fixed allowable shear stress, about 4012 psi for a main power-transmitting shaft and 8447 psi for a short shaft. That is why the short-shaft equation is less conservative, and it let me check the Handbook result against the torsion formula.
  • The shafts were already over-sized. The input shaft had a safety factor of 3.3 and the output shaft 20.7, so the 1 in long shafts at these diameters were not the limiting part of the design.
  • Bearing size comes from the stack-up. The bearing inside diameter is set by the shaft, and the length is limited by the 1 in shaft: bearing + gap + bearing has to equal the shaft length.
  • The lecture bearing load uses only the radial force. The tangential force (2.500 lbf) is larger than the radial force (0.910 lbf), so the real load on the bearings is closer to the resultant tooth force of 2.66 lbf. Porous bronze still passes with a wide margin, but a plastic with a PV limit near 3000 would have little room to spare.
  • Strength was not what set the bearing wall. The strength minimum was about 0.0002 in, far below anything that can be made, so the wall comes from standard sizes, and McMaster lists the bearing OD as the housing ID, which sets the bore.
  • Check each sketch against its own labels. My plate sketches labeled the bearing holes at (±0.344, 0), side by side, but I had drawn them stacked vertically. The CAD uses the side-by-side layout from the coordinates, and both plates are viewed from the same side so the holes line up.
  • The spacer length comes from the stack-up, not from the shaft length. My first item list had 1 in spacers. The spacers set the 0.500 in gap between the plates (the cavity variable), not the 1 in shaft length.
  • No room for snap rings. I first sketched snap ring grooves on the shafts. The two plates and the gap already use the full 1 in shaft length (0.25 + 0.50 + 0.25), so there is no length left for external rings. The shafts are plain, and washers hold the gears in position axially.
  • Every washer needs a location and an ID. A list of washers by thickness alone does not say which shaft or which side of the gear each one goes on, and the ID has to clear that shaft.
  • One equations file keeps the parts in sync. Linking both plates to the same external file meant the center distance, plate size, and hole positions stay consistent, and a change in one place updates both plates.
  • Use the exact value, not the displayed one. The equations window shows CD/2 as 0.34 in and bore_gear as 0.38 in because of rounding, but the model holds 0.34375 in and 0.375 in. The sketch value of 0.344 is only a rounded label.
  • Dimension names differ between parts. The screw hole sketch is Sketch5 on plate A and Sketch3 on plate B, so the equation names have to be picked from each part instead of copied from one to the other.
  • Handwriting has to be read by someone else. A handwritten 3 in N1 reads like a 9 in the scan, so I added a note under the scan.

Mistakes

  • Hand calculations: none; every result matched the check values.
  • Hole layout. The plate sketches drew the bearing holes stacked vertically while the labeled coordinates put them side by side. The CAD uses the side-by-side layout.
  • Spacer length. The item list gave the spacers as 1 in long. They need to be 0.500 in to leave the correct gap between the plates, and the CAD uses the cavity variable.
  • Snap ring grooves. The shaft sketches called for grooves for snap rings, which do not fit because the plates and the gap already use the full 1 in shaft length. They were left out of the CAD.
  • Washer list. The washers were listed by thickness only, without their location and inside diameter on each shaft.

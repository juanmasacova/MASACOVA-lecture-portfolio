A10 – Lead Screw Translating System

Objective

This is part one of a two-part team assignment with Ethan King: the conceptual design of a purely mechanical tensile tester. The machine pulls a specimen with up to 2 kN of axial tension while a crosshead moves at a controllable 1–50 mm/min, and it has no electronics and no software. Two identical lead screws, one on each side of the frame, move the crosshead, and the design safety factor is 4. Part one has six pieces of work: size the lead screws (diameter, rotational speed, and torque) and select them, select a bronze nut and flange, design the crosshead for strength and for a maximum deflection of 0.1 mm, tabulate the cost of purchased materials, and assemble the machine in CAD with the frame, screws, nuts, and crosshead.

1. Minimum Leadscrew Diameter: Ethan King

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: F = 2 kN = 450 lbf, n = 4, Sy = 250 MPa, two screws. Unknowns: load per screw Fs, allowable stress σallow, minimum area Amin, and minimum diameter dmin.

ii. Free body diagram. One leadscrew, loaded in axial tension by Fs = F/2 through the nut, with the frame bearing supplying the equal and opposite reaction.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Load per screw: Fs = F/2.
  • Allowable stress: σallow = Sy/n.
  • Axial stress on a round bar, σ = Fs/A with A = πd2/4. At the minimum size σ = σallow, so Amin = Fs/σallow and dmin = √(4Fs/(πσallow)).

iv. Numerical solution.

2. Range of Rotational Speed: Ethan King

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: crosshead speed v = 1 to 50 mm/min and lead L = 0.25 in = 6.35 mm per revolution. Unknowns: rotational speed N at each end of the range.

ii. Free body diagram. This is a motion problem and no force enters it, so the sketch is kinematic: the screw turns at N and the nut and crosshead advance at v.

iii. Model and algebraic solution. The nut advances one lead per revolution, so v = N·L and N = v/L. The lead is the travel per turn, which is the number of thread starts times the pitch. The selected screw has two starts at a pitch of 0.125 in, so its lead is 0.25 in.

Thread Bending Stress in the Nut

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: load per screw F = 1 kN = 224.8 lbf, lead = 0.25 in with 2 starts, 8 threads per inch, nominal diameter D = 0.75 in, nut length Le = 1.25 in (fully screwed on), Sy = 49,000 psi for 673 bronze, n = 4. Unknowns: pitch p, number of engaged threads nt, minimum minor diameter dmin, allowable stress σmax, and calculated bending stress σbend.

ii. Free body diagram. A section through the engaged threads, with the load acting on the thread flanks at the mean diameter dm and the thread thickness p/2 marked at the root (Ethan's sketch, below).

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Pitch: p = lead/2 for two starts, checked with p = 1/TPI.
  • Engaged threads: nt = Le/p.
  • Minimum minor diameter of a Class 2C internal thread, from the Machinery's Handbook formula (formula 11): dmin = D − 0.9p.
  • Allowable stress: σmax = Sy/n.
  • Thread bending stress: σbend = 6F/(πntdminp).

Two notes: (1) dmin is the smallest diameter of the nut's threads, so it makes the stress slightly higher than a root diameter would, which is conservative. 6. Crosshead: Juan

The crosshead is modeled as a simply supported beam. The two nut flanges are the supports, so each carries half of the load, and the specimen grip pulls down at midspan. The crosshead's own weight (about 21 N) and shear deflection are neglected. The safety factor of 4 is applied to strength, and the 0.1 mm deflection limit is checked at the full 2 kN.

9. Lessons Learned: Juan

Stiffness sized the crosshead, not strength. The bending stress calculation needed a thickness of only 12.2 mm, while the 0.1 mm deflection limit needed 25.0 mm, so deflection set the size. The 1 1/4 in bar I chose carries 9.9 MPa against an allowable 67.5 MPa, an actual safety factor of 27, because it was sized to be stiff. I learned to run both checks, strength with the safety factor of 4 and deflection at the full load, and to size the part by whichever one gives the larger thickness.

Span and thickness both enter the deflection formula as cubes. For a simply supported beam with a center load, δ = FL3/(4Ebh3). With the same 1 1/4 in bar, a 10 in span deflects 0.049 mm and Instron's 16.5 in column spacing would deflect 0.219 mm, 4.5 times as much. Going from a 1 in bar to a 1 1/4 in bar cuts the deflection from 0.095 mm to 0.049 mm. Choosing the span from research on real machines, and then the thickness, gave a design that met the limit with margin.

The catalog data of a purchased part sets the design of the part around it. The 2.625 in flange diameter set the minimum crosshead width. The nut's 1.125 in body, 3/4 in long, set the 1 3/16 in by 3/4 in counterbore. The 2.094 in bolt circle and the 17/64 in holes set the flange mounting holes. I also learned to check edge distance: holes at 12, 3, 6, and 9 o'clock leave 0.32 in of material to the bar edge, and rotating the pattern 45° leaves 0.63 in, so the CAD uses the 45° pattern.

Equations tie dimensions to design intent. I defined the bar length as Span + FlangeDia + 2 × EndMargin and the end distance as (Length − Span)/2, so changing the span changes the bar length and the hole spacing together instead of requiring me to edit each dimension. I also added EdgeDist = Width/2 − BoltCircle/2 − BoltHoleDia/2 as a check value that is not used by any feature.

The torque formula uses the mean thread diameter of the selected screw. The first torque calculation used the 4.6 mm minimum rod diameter from the stress check and gave 1.45 N·m. For the 3/4 in–8 Acme screw the mean diameter is 17.46 mm, which gives 2.36 N·m, and 2.41 N·m with the thread-angle correction μ/cos 14.5°. I learned to carry the dimensions of the part actually selected into each later calculation, and to check the units of every result, such as writing a moment in N·mm instead of N/mm.

A nut has to match the screw's lead, not only its diameter. A 3/4 in–8 nut with 1 thread start travels 0.125 in per turn and cannot thread onto the screw's 2 starts and 0.25 in per turn. Checking the thread starts and the travel per turn in the catalog before ordering avoids that mismatch. The correct nut is also rated for 2,800 lbf of dynamic thrust against 225 lbf per nut, which confirmed the thread bending check.

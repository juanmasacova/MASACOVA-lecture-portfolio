A9 – Pulleys

Objective

The objective is to design a belt-and-pulley drive that transfers power between two parallel shafts 4–6 in apart. Both pulleys are 3 in in diameter, the speed is 1000–2000 rpm, the static belt tension is 4–6 lbf, and the minimum safety factor is 3. The belt is polyurethane rubber with a yield strength of 3.25 MPa (471 psi) at 20% strain. The work has four parts: select a round belt from the forces on its tight and slack sides, size the shafts for torsion in hardened 1060 steel, design the journal bearings from pressure, velocity, and PV rating, and design a frame that holds the pulley system. The pulley comes last in the chain of decisions: I calculated which belt I need, selected a standard size that keeps the safety factor above 3 with margin, and then sized the pulley groove to that belt.

Given Data and Assumptions

The values below are either given by the assignment or chosen inside the range it allows. Every load that follows (belt tension, shaft torque, bearing load, frame force) grows with the static tension and the speed, so I chose the top of both ranges. That way the finished design holds anywhere in the allowed window.

  • There is no slippage. The belt is evaluated at the impending-slip limit, where friction is fully used, which gives the largest tight-side tension the belt will ever carry for a given static tension.
  • The groove is rounded and matches the belt, so the plain friction coefficient applies with no wedging effect.
  • The two pulleys are equal and the belt is open, so the wrap angle is θ = π on both pulleys whatever the center distance is.
  • The belt stress is uniform normal stress, σ = T1/A.
  • The belt centerline rides at the 3 in diameter. The groove geometry in the pulley section shows this is about 1.2% high.
  • The belt length stays constant, so the average of the two tensions, with the centrifugal tension removed, stays at the static tension. This is the standard belt-drive relation, from Hijazi's Chapter 17 notes (p. 3 for the tension relation, p. 4 for the belting equation).

1. Selecting the Belt

Forces on the Tight and Slack Sides, Belt Area, and Diameter

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: D = 3 in, r = 1.5 in, C = 5 in (a starting value, refined below), N = 2000 rpm, T0 = 6 lbf, n = 3, Sy = 471 psi, μ = 0.8, θ = π, ρ = 0.04335 lbm/in3, g = 386.1 in/s2. From these: ω = 2πN/60 = 209.44 rad/s, v = ωr = 314.16 in/s, and σallow = Sy/n = 157 psi. Unknowns: tight-side tension T1, slack-side tension T2, centrifugal tension Tc, transmitted torque T, shaft reaction R, belt area A, belt diameter db, and belt outer circumference Lout.

ii. Free body diagram. Three sketches. In the belt drive, both pulleys turn counterclockwise. The top strand runs into the driver pulley, so it carries the tight-side tension T1, and the bottom strand carries T2. On the driver pulley alone, T1 and T2 pull toward the driven pulley at radius r, the input torque T turns the pulley, and the shaft pushes back with the reaction R. On a small belt element of angle dφ, the tensions T and T + dT act along the two ends, the pulley pushes outward with the normal force dN, the friction force μdN acts along the belt toward the lower-tension side, and the centrifugal force Tcdφ acts outward.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Driver pulley: T = r(T1 − T2) from the moments about the shaft, and R = T1 + T2 from the forces.
  • Belt element, radial: dN + Tcdφ = T dφ, so dN = (T − Tc)dφ. Tangential: dT = μdN. Combining them gives dT/(T − Tc) = μdφ, and integrating from T2 to T1 over the wrap angle gives (T1 − Tc)/(T2 − Tc) = eμθ = E.
  • Static tension: (T1 − Tc) + (T2 − Tc) = 2T0. Solving with the belting equation gives T1 − Tc = 2T0E/(E + 1) and T2 − Tc = 2T0/(E + 1).
  • Centrifugal tension: σc = ρv2/g and Tc = σcA, since the mass per length is ρA.
  • Normal stress and yield: at the minimum size, T1 = σallowA. Writing T1 = (T1 − Tc) + σcA gives Amin = [2T0E/(E + 1)] / (σallow − σc) and dmin = √(4Amin/π).
  • Torque: T = r(T1 − T2) = 2T0r(E − 1)/(E + 1). The centrifugal tension cancels in the difference.

iv. Numerical solution.

Belt Selection

The belt has to be at least 0.311 in thick, so I checked the standard round belt diameters at and above that size. A thicker belt is heavier, so its centrifugal tension grows with its area. For each candidate I recomputed the safety factor with SF = Sy/(T1′/A + σc), where T1′ = T1 − Tc = 11.10 lbf. I also checked an upper bound that holds for any friction coefficient. T2 − Tc cannot go below zero, so T1 can never exceed 2T0 + Tc.

The smallest size that reaches SF = 3 is 5/16 in, but it passes by less than 1% and falls below 3 at the upper bound, so a modest error in μ or ρ would break it. The 3/8 in belt passes both checks with room to spare, and the 1/2 in belt would be heavier than the design needs. I selected the 3/8 in belt: McMaster-Carr 3044K608, a clear polyurethane rubber round belt, 3/8 in in diameter and 20 in in outer circumference, rated for −10°F to 150°F.

Circumference and Center Distance

For an open belt on two equal pulleys, the two straight spans are each C long and the wraps add up to πD. The outer surface of the loop is longer than the centerline by πdb, because the belt turns through a total of 2π. So Lout = 2C + πD + πdb. With C = 5 in and the 3/8 in belt this is 10 + 9.42 + 1.18 = 20.60 in.

The stocked belt is 20 in around, so it is shorter than the loop and stretches to produce the static tension. The belt stiffness is not given, so I estimated it from the yield data as a secant modulus, Es = Sy/εy = 471/0.20 = 2355 psi. The strain that gives T0 = 6 lbf is ε0 = (T0/A)/Es = (6/0.1104)/2355 = 2.31%, so the installed loop must be 20(1.0231) = 20.46 in around.

  • Solving the circumference equation for the center distance: C = (Lout − πD − πdb)/2 = (20.46 − 9.425 − 1.178)/2 = 4.93 in, which is inside the allowed 4–6 in.
  • Keeping C = 5 in would stretch the belt 3.0% and give T0 = A Es ε = 7.8 lbf, above the allowed 4–6 lbf. With C = 4.93 in the strain is 2.31% and T0 = 6.0 lbf.
  • The wrap angle stays π, so none of the belt forces above change with C. The 4.93 in only sets the frame length.
  • The secant modulus is an estimate, so the 6 lbf is approximate. A real belt would be tensioned by measuring it.

2. Sizing the Shaft

The driver shaft carries the belt torque from the input end to the pulley, so it is loaded in pure torsion. The bending from the belt pull is handled by the bearing supports in the next section, so here I size the shaft for torque only, as the assignment asks.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: T = 15.30 in·lbf (from section 1), n = 3, Sut = 250 ksi for hardened 1060-class steel (53 HRC, MakeItFrom, Hardened H 1.1060 steel), and the ratio k = Sy/Sut = 0.5. I did not find a yield strength for this exact heat treatment, so k = 0.5 is an assumed, conservative ratio; the sensitivity is shown below. Unknowns: Sy, τy, τallow, polar section modulus Zp = J/c, dmin, Amin, and for the chosen shaft the stress τ and the safety factor.

ii. Free body diagram. The shaft is loaded by the input torque T at the input end and the equal and opposite torque from the pulley, so the shaft is in equilibrium with a constant internal torque T along its length. On the cross section, the shear stress is zero at the center and grows linearly to τmax at the surface, radius c = d/2.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Yield: Sy = k·Sut. Maximum shear stress theory gives τy = Sy/2.
  • Allowable: τallow = τy/n.
  • Torsion: τmax = Tc/J with J = πd4/32 and c = d/2, so τmax = 16T/(πd3).
  • Minimum size: Zp = J/c = πd3/16 = T/τallow, so dmin = (16T/(πτallow))1/3 and Amin = πdmin2/4.

iv. Numerical solution.

3. Journal Bearing for the Shaft

The shaft from section 2 turns in two journal bearings, one on each side of the pulley. This section finds the pressure, sliding velocity, and PV value on one bearing and checks them against the limits of the sintered metal and non-metallic bearing materials in Machinery's Handbook, Table 4, p. 2440.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: D = 0.25 in (the section 2 shaft), N = 2000 rpm, T1 = 12.32 lbf and T2 = 2.12 lbf (section 1), and a bearing length Lb = 0.25 in, which is the standard stocked length and the same length I used in A08. There are two identical bearings. Unknowns: shaft load R, bearing load Fb, pressure P, velocity V, PV, and the bearing material.

ii. Free body diagram. Two sketches. In the side view of the shaft, the belt load R acts downward on the pulley, and each bearing pushes up with R/2. This assumes the pulley sits midway between the bearings, so the two supports share the load equally. In the bearing sketch, the shaft pushes on the bearing with Fb = R/2, and the bearing carries it as a pressure over the projected area D·Lb.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Shaft equilibrium: R = T1 + T2 and, by symmetry, Fb = R/2.
  • Pressure on the projected area: P = Fb/(D·Lb).
  • Sliding velocity at the shaft surface: V = πDN/12 in ft/min, with D in inches and N in rpm.
  • PV = P·V, compared with the limit of each material.

4. Frame

The frame holds the two shafts 4.93 in apart. I modeled it as two identical flat link plates, one on each side of the pulleys, each with two bearing holes. The belt pulls the two pulleys toward each other, so each plate is squeezed between its two holes. This section checks the plate for strength at the holes and for buckling.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: C = 4.93 in, F = R/2 = 7.22 lbf at each hole (each plate carries half of the belt load R = 14.44 lbf), hole diameter d = 0.375 in (the housing bore of the McMaster-Carr bearing from section 3), plate width w = 1.00 in, thickness t = 0.25 in, aluminum 6061-T6 with Sy = 40 ksi and E = 10 × 106 psi, n = 3, and a stress concentration factor Kt = 3 at the hole. Unknowns: σallow, net-section stress σ, bearing stress σb, buckling load Pcr, and the safety factors.

ii. Free body diagram. Front view: the plate with a load F at each hole pointing toward the other hole, so the section between the holes is in compression. Cut view: a section through a hole, where the force F acts on the net area (w − d)t, and the bearing presses on the hole over the area d·t.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • σallow = Sy/n
  • Net section at the hole: σ = KtF/((w − d)t).
  • Bearing stress on the hole wall: σb = F/(d·t).
  • Buckling of the strut between the holes, with pinned ends: Pcr = π2EI/C2, with I = wt3/12 about the weak axis.
  • Safety factors: Sy/σ, Sy/σb, and Pcr/F.

5. Lessons Learned

  • Centrifugal tension matters at 2000 rpm. The belt moves at 314 in/s, which adds σc = 11.08 psi, 7% of the 157 psi allowable stress. Leaving it out gives a minimum diameter of 0.300 in instead of 0.311 in. That 0.011 in decided the belt choice: the 5/16 in belt passed SF = 3 by less than 1% and dropped below 3 at the top of the center distance range, so I selected the 3/8 in belt.
  • Center distance sets the belt tension. The stocked 20 in belt is a fixed length, so the center distance decides how much it stretches. At C = 5 in the stretched belt would carry about 7.8 lbf of static tension, above the 6 lbf limit, so I reduced C to 4.93 in. A 0.07 in change in C moved the tension by about 1.8 lbf, so the drive has to be sized around the belt and not the other way around.
  • The pulley material changes the torque. With μ = 0.8 on aluminum, E = eμθ = 12.35. With μ = 0.7 (polyurethane on steel) it would be 9.02, and the torque at the same static tension drops from 15.30 to 14.40 in·lbf, about 6%. The friction coefficient had to come from a source for the exact material pair, not a general value.
  • The shaft is not strength-limited; the standard size is. The minimum diameter is 0.155 in and the 1/4 in shaft gives SF = 12.5. I chose 1/4 in because it matches the pulley bore and the stocked bearing. I could not find a yield strength for this exact hardened 1060, so I assumed Sy = 0.5Sut. A ratio of 0.9 only changes the minimum to 0.128 in, so the result does not depend on that assumption.
  • Speed drives the bearing, not load. The bearing load is only 7.22 lbf, but at 2000 rpm PV is 15,120, about 11 times the 1,398 in A08. That eliminated acetal, nylon, Teflon, graphite, phenolics, and wood, and left only porous bronze and porous iron. The PV margin for bronze is 3.3 times, and a 1/2 in bearing would double it because P and PV scale with 1/Lb.
  • The direction of the load decides the failure mode. The belt pulls the two pulleys toward each other, so the frame plate is in compression and not tension. That is why I checked buckling (Pcr = 5,288 lbf, SF = 732) as well as the net-section stress (SF = 289). The frame is far stronger than it needs to be, so the plate size came from the 1/4 in bearing length (t = 0.25 in) and the 0.31 in of material beside each hole.
  • Calculate first, then shop. I had started looking at stocked belts before calculating the minimum diameter and the forces. Doing the calculation first gave the size window (0.311 in minimum, with the 5/16 and 3/8 in belts as candidates), so the stock list could be checked against it in one pass.

Mistakes

  • I started to select a belt from the stock listings before calculating the forces and minimum diameter. The assignment order is forces, then diameter, then circumference, then selection, so I went back and did the calculation first.
  • The first pulley I found was UHMW polyethylene, which is not one of the three allowed pulley materials, and its friction coefficient is not the one used in the analysis. I used it only as a geometry template (groove size and depth) and sized an aluminum pulley instead.
  • I first used C = 5 in for the center distance. With the 20 in belt it stretched to about 7.8 lbf of static tension, above the 6 lbf limit, so I refined it to 4.93 in.
  • I did not find a yield strength for hardened 1060 steel in MatWeb or McMaster, so I used a sourced tensile strength and an assumed yield ratio, and checked how sensitive the shaft size is to it.

A9 – Pulleys

Objective

The objective is to design a belt-and-pulley drive that transfers power between two parallel shafts 4–6 in apart. Both pulleys are 3 in in diameter, the speed is 1000–2000 rpm, the static belt tension is 4–6 lbf, and the minimum safety factor is 3. The belt is polyurethane rubber with a yield strength of 3.25 MPa (471 psi) at 20% strain. The work has four parts: select a round belt from the forces on its tight and slack sides, size the shafts for torsion in hardened 1060 steel, design the journal bearings from pressure, velocity, and PV rating, and design a frame that holds the pulley system. The pulley comes last in the chain of decisions: I calculated which belt I need, selected a standard size that keeps the safety factor above 3 with margin, and then sized the pulley groove to that belt.

Given Data and Assumptions

The values below are either given by the assignment or chosen inside the range it allows. Every load that follows (belt tension, shaft torque, bearing load, frame force) grows with the static tension and the speed, so I chose the top of both ranges. That way the finished design holds anywhere in the allowed window.

1. Selecting the Belt

Forces on the Tight and Slack Sides, Belt Area, and Diameter

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: D = 3 in, r = 1.5 in, C = 5 in (a starting value, refined below), N = 2000 rpm, T0 = 6 lbf, n = 3, Sy = 471 psi, μ = 0.8, θ = π, ρ = 0.04335 lbm/in3, g = 386.1 in/s2. From these: ω = 2πN/60 = 209.44 rad/s, v = ωr = 314.16 in/s, and σallow = Sy/n = 157 psi. Unknowns: tight-side tension T1, slack-side tension T2, centrifugal tension Tc, transmitted torque T, shaft reaction R, belt area A, belt diameter db, and belt outer circumference Lout.

ii. Free body diagram. Three sketches. In the belt drive, both pulleys turn counterclockwise. The top strand runs into the driver pulley, so it carries the tight-side tension T1, and the bottom strand carries T2. On the driver pulley alone, T1 and T2 pull toward the driven pulley at radius r, the input torque T turns the pulley, and the shaft pushes back with the reaction R. On a small belt element of angle dφ, the tensions T and T + dT act along the two ends, the pulley pushes outward with the normal force dN, the friction force μdN acts along the belt toward the lower-tension side, and the centrifugal force Tcdφ acts outward.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Driver pulley: T = r(T1 − T2) from the moments about the shaft, and R = T1 + T2 from the forces.
  • Belt element, radial: dN + Tcdφ = T dφ, so dN = (T − Tc)dφ. Tangential: dT = μdN. Combining them gives dT/(T − Tc) = μdφ, and integrating from T2 to T1 over the wrap angle gives (T1 − Tc)/(T2 − Tc) = eμθ = E.
  • Static tension: (T1 − Tc) + (T2 − Tc) = 2T0. Solving with the belting equation gives T1 − Tc = 2T0E/(E + 1) and T2 − Tc = 2T0/(E + 1).
  • Centrifugal tension: σc = ρv2/g and Tc = σcA, since the mass per length is ρA.
  • Normal stress and yield: at the minimum size, T1 = σallowA. Writing T1 = (T1 − Tc) + σcA gives Amin = [2T0E/(E + 1)] / (σallow − σc) and dmin = √(4Amin/π).
  • Torque: T = r(T1 − T2) = 2T0r(E − 1)/(E + 1). The centrifugal tension cancels in the difference.

Belt Selection

The belt has to be at least 0.311 in thick, so I checked the standard round belt diameters at and above that size. A thicker belt is heavier, so its centrifugal tension grows with its area. For each candidate I recomputed the safety factor with SF = Sy/(T1′/A + σc), where T1′ = T1 − Tc = 11.10 lbf. I also checked an upper bound that holds for any friction coefficient. T2 − Tc cannot go below zero, so T1 can never exceed 2T0 + Tc.

Circumference and Center Distance

For an open belt on two equal pulleys, the two straight spans are each C long and the wraps add up to πD. The outer surface of the loop is longer than the centerline by πdb, because the belt turns through a total of 2π. So Lout = 2C + πD + πdb. With C = 5 in and the 3/8 in belt this is 10 + 9.42 + 1.18 = 20.60 in.

The stocked belt is 20 in around, so it is shorter than the loop and stretches to produce the static tension. The belt stiffness is not given, so I estimated it from the yield data as a secant modulus, Es = Sy/εy = 471/0.20 = 2355 psi. The strain that gives T0 = 6 lbf is ε0 = (T0/A)/Es = (6/0.1104)/2355 = 2.31%, so the installed loop must be 20(1.0231) = 20.46 in around.

  • Solving the circumference equation for the center distance: C = (Lout − πD − πdb)/2 = (20.46 − 9.425 − 1.178)/2 = 4.93 in, which is inside the allowed 4–6 in.
  • Keeping C = 5 in would stretch the belt 3.0% and give T0 = A Es ε = 7.8 lbf, above the allowed 4–6 lbf. With C = 4.93 in the strain is 2.31% and T0 = 6.0 lbf.
  • The wrap angle stays π, so none of the belt forces above change with C. The 4.93 in only sets the frame length.
  • The secant modulus is an estimate, so the 6 lbf is approximate. A real belt would be tensioned by measuring it.

2. Sizing the Shaft

The driver shaft carries the belt torque from the input end to the pulley, so it is loaded in pure torsion. The bending from the belt pull is handled by the bearing supports in the next section, so here I size the shaft for torque only, as the assignment asks.

i. Knowns and unknowns. Knowns: T = 15.30 in·lbf (from section 1), n = 3, Sut = 250 ksi for hardened 1060-class steel (53 HRC, MakeItFrom, Hardened H 1.1060 steel), and the ratio k = Sy/Sut = 0.5. I did not find a yield strength for this exact heat treatment, so k = 0.5 is an assumed, conservative ratio; the sensitivity is shown below. Unknowns: Sy, τy, τallow, polar section modulus Zp = J/c, dmin, Amin, and for the chosen shaft the stress τ and the safety factor.

ii. Free body diagram. The shaft is loaded by the input torque T at the input end and the equal and opposite torque from the pulley, so the shaft is in equilibrium with a constant internal torque T along its length. On the cross section, the shear stress is zero at the center and grows linearly to τmax at the surface, radius c = d/2.

iii. Model and algebraic solution.

  • Yield: Sy = k·Sut. Maximum shear stress theory gives τy = Sy/2.
  • Allowable: τallow = τy/n.
  • Torsion: τmax = Tc/J with J = πd4/32 and c = d/2, so τmax = 16T/(πd3).
  • Minimum size: Zp = J/c = πd3/16 = T/τallow, so dmin = (16T/(πτallow))1/3 and Amin = πdmin2/4.

v. Answer. The minimum shaft diameter is 0.155 in. I chose a 1/4 in shaft, the standard size that also matches the 1/4 in bore of the pulley, which gives a shear stress of 4,987 psi and a safety factor of 12.5, well above the required 3. A 3/16 in shaft would also pass (safety factor 5.3), but it leaves less margin for the bending that the bearing section adds. If the yield ratio were as high as k = 0.9, dmin would be 0.128 in, so the 1/4 in shaft is safe over the whole plausible range of the assumption.

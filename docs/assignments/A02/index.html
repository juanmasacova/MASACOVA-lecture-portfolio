A2 – Truss Stress Analysis

Objective

The objective is to design and analyze a lightweight 6061-T6 aluminum planar truss, using FBDs, safety-factored area and pin sizing. Also use symbolic/numerical calculations to size members and pins, then validate the design by building a CAD model and comparing its weight against hand calculations.

Analyze

The first step is to figure out what information I know about the problem. The truss will be connected to A, B, C, D. Point A is a pin and point B is a roller. In the image below, the dimensions are a = 0.4m, b = 0.3m and P = 30kN.

So I copied the image into Windows Paint and sketched a simple structure connecting the points, while restricting the angles of freedom that a roller and a pin support have. This way achieving a completely static structure. I made a triangle in the middle to further support the weight in C and D, which required adding point F (rigid connection). See picture below.

Now below I will:

  1. Sketch the truss and find the lengths of each bar.
  2. Sketch a FBD of each joint in the truss.
  3. Symbolically solve for internal forces.
  4. Numerically solve for internal forces.

Now I'm instructed to use the largest internal force to calculate the required cross-sectional area of the bars using a safety factor of 3.5, and the yield strength. I will be calculating this using aluminum 6061-T6, with SolidWorks given values for the material. The work will be shown in the image below, following these steps:

  1. A list of all the knowns and unknowns.
  2. Symbolical solution for minimum cross-sectional area.
  3. Numerical solution for the cross-sectional area.
  4. The approximate weight of the truss.

Now I have to determine the cross-sectional area of the connecting pins, which are made of hardened tool steel with a yield shear strength of 170 ksi and a density of 0.278 lb/in3. I am instructed to assume that elements that are in compression won't fail in buckling. I will paste my work below. The steps are the following:

  1. A list of all the knowns, quantities and unknowns.
  2. FBD of pin A, which is the pin with the largest reaction load.
  3. Symbolical solution for minimum cross-sectional area.
  4. Numerical solution for the cross-sectional area.
  5. Determine the approximate combined weight of the pins.

Now is finally time to 3D model the truss and pins. I will use SolidWorks student edition. I will start by designing the truss as a single part and then the pins as another part, assigning the correct material to each. Then I will make an assembly with the truss and the 5 pins and get a weight estimate with the weight evaluation tool in SolidWorks.

First I open a sketch and using the front plane I add and fully constraint points A, B, C, D and F, using F as the origin. They are constrained by dimensions and horizontal constraints for B, F, A and for C, D.

Then I drew construction lines connecting the points…

I calculated earlier that the bars should be 25.23mm X 25.23mm. I used the offset entities option, selected all construction lines, toggled bi-directional so that the offset is equal on both sides of the construction line, and made the offset dimension 25.23mm/2.

Then trimmed any overlapping lines so that I could get the bar closed geometry.

I saved the sketch. Then I clicked on the sketch and then clicked extrude. I extruded it 25.23mm so that all the bars are now 25.23mm X 25.23mm.

I finally have the truss!!! This is taking forever…

Now I have to make space for the pins. I calculated their diameter at 11.42mm. Here I could sketch the pin holes over the truss and cut the sketch, but I think it will be easier to edit the initial sketch since I already have the points sketched. So that's what I'll do. I'll draw circles at each previously marked point, dimension 1 of them to 11.42mm and then constraint all the other circles to equal size. It looks like this.

Now it's time to design the pins, this should be easy. They are just cylinders with a diameter of 11.42mm and I'll extrude them to a length of 35.23mm so that they come out of the truss 5mm each side. I'll start by opening a new part on SolidWorks, then clicking the sketch and selecting the front plane again (so when I make the assembly it's already at the right angle). I'll make a 11.42mm circle using the center as the origin.

Then exit the sketch and click extrude. Here I will extrude it to 35.23mm as I said earlier.

And the final product looks something like this.

Now I will put them together in an assembly and figure out the weight. I start by going back to the truss and then going to file→Make drawing from part. A new tab opened asking where I want to put the truss, I clicked the green tick mark so that it is placed at the origin. Once placed, I went to the "Basic Assembly Tools" tab and clicked on "Insert Components". I browsed my files for the pin and opened it in the assembly, then placed it somewhere random for now. I clicked the pin and copy-pasted it 4 times so that I have 5 pins floating around.

Then I clicked "Mate" and selected the wall of one of the pins and the inside of one of the pin holes, and mated them using a concentric mate. Then I clicked one of the ends of that same pin and the wall of the truss and added a distance mate of 5mm. I will repeat this process for all 5 pins. It looks like this.

I was about to evaluate weight but I remembered that I didn't add any material to the parts, so I will do that right now. First I'll open the truss part and on the reference tree I will right click on material, then edit material, and on the menu that popped up I searched for 6061-T6 aluminum and clicked it, then hit "apply" and exit the menu.

Now I will do the same for the pin. I open the part, right click material, edit material, then search for hardened tool steel. I couldn't find a tool steel that had the exact properties I used to calculate the weight earlier, but I found something similar although a bit more dense called AISI Type A2 Tool Steel. It doesn't bother me because the weight of the pins is so small compared to the whole system that I don't think is going to make much of a difference. So I selected it and hit apply.

Now I go back to the assembly and it is finally time to evaluate weight. Lets remember I calculated earlier that the truss weighs 5.71kg and the pins a combined weight of 0.045kg which adds up to 5.76kg. To evaluate the weight of my design in SolidWorks I go to the "Evaluate" tab and click "Mass Properties". Here I see that SolidWorks calculated my assembly weight at 5.70kg. Not bad, off by about 60g or 1.05%.

One lesson learned from this project is that for an axially loaded member, cross-sectional shape does not affect the strength calculation, since stress is force divided by area regardless of geometry, which is why a square, round, or tubular cross-section of equal area all do the same. Shape only becomes relevant when buckling of a compression member is considered, which in this case, wasn't.

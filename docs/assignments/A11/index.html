A11 – Motor Selection

Objective

This is part two of the team assignment with Ethan King and Josef Chelala: the motorized drive and structural integration of the 2 kN purely mechanical tensile tester from A10. Part two covers selecting a motor, designing the motor mount, selecting a coupler, designing a frame that supports the crosshead and the tensile load, designing the clamp, a complete CAD model, and a bill of materials with engineering cost. The constraints are a 2 kN load, a 1–50 mm/min speed range, an 18 in minimum leadscrew length, and a safety factor of 4.

1. Motor Selection: Ethan King

The motor was selected from the torque and speed requirements of the leadscrew design and checked at the maximum axial load of 2 kN across the whole 1–50 mm/min range.

Selected motor: McMaster-Carr 6627T117, a stepper motor with driver (48 V DC, 4.3 A, 1/10 stepping).

Torque verification. The maximum required torque is 2.93 N·m (2.161 lbf-ft). The motor's torque at low speed is 637 ozf-in, which is 3.318 lbf-ft, so the motor is well above what is needed.

The speed range of 1–50 mm/min needs only a few rpm at the leadscrew, where the motor torque is highest, so it provides the required torque across the whole range.

Speed-torque curve of the selected motor.

Motor specification screenshots are on Ethan's page: Ethan King's A11.

2. Motor Mount Bracket: Ethan King

The bracket attaches the motor to the top of the frame using the motor's own mounting-hole pattern and a four-hole pattern into the frame. It keeps the motor aligned with the leadscrew, leaves room for assembly and maintenance, and carries the operating torque and reaction forces. The final bracket was modeled in CAD with the calculated thickness and hole locations.

3. Coupler: Ethan King

Selected coupling: McMaster-Carr 6412K14. It connects the motor shaft to the leadscrew, and torsional shear stress analysis was used to confirm that it carries the maximum torque of 2.93 N·m.

4. Frame Analysis and Design: Josef Chelala

Reaction forces

Seen from the top, only the motor torques act on the top frame. The reactions sit at the ends, so the length of the top frame resists the torque and the transverse force on each vertical frame is only 1.525 lbf. Each leadscrew also pulls down 1 kN, so the top frame pushes 1 kN of compression into each vertical frame.

Bracket selection

With the A10 beams, the top frame limits the bracket to under 38.1 mm wide and 41.275 mm long, for an 8.128 mm slot.

Two brackets fit. The gusseted bracket costs more and handles more bending, but the loads are small, so we use the cheaper 5JRP7 aluminum inside-corner bracket without a gusset ($6.84 against $9.99).

Top frame FEA (17 in, 40 series)

Setup. The faces in contact with the vertical frames and brackets were split and fixed. The vertical frame contacts only have vertical fixtures. The motor torque is 2.16 lbf-ft (25.932 lbf-in) and a 1 kN vertical force is applied at each leadscrew hole. The mesh is the center setting.

Final simulation setup.

Von Mises stress. The maximum is 20.159 ksi around the leadscrew holes, against a yield strength of about 27.992 ksi. This is probably overstated, because the model does not include support from the leadscrew itself and the fixtures are idealized. With a safety factor of 4 the allowable stress is 6.998 ksi, and only small areas near the hole and along the bracket line exceed it. The load path runs into the screw holes, as if the frame were being punched there.

Deflection. The maximum deflection is 0.001616 in, at one screw hole where the 1 kN force and the torque combine. That is half the thickness of a human hair (0.003 in). The exaggerated plot looks like a four-point bend test, with little movement at the supports and no visible twisting.

Factor of safety. The smallest factor of safety is 1.386, in a tiny region. Plotting only the areas below 4 shows that only small spots are affected.

Optimization. Some of the low spots are at indents in the extrusion near the screw hole. Welding those gaps acts as a retainer and removes the stresses below the factor of safety of 4. The next-lowest area moves to another indent, so custom top frames with fewer indents would be stronger. Deflection rose slightly but stayed in the same area.

Vertical frame FEA

Setup. The whole bottom face is fixed because it sits on the base beam. The 1.525 lbf transverse force is applied where the bracket touches the frame and the 1 kN compression where the top frame touches it.

Results. The maximum stress is 2.516 ksi, at the center of the top-frame contact, far below yield. The maximum deflection is 0.0003796 in at the point where the top frame, side frame, and bracket meet. The minimum factor of safety is 11.124, almost three times the target of 4, and there is no visible twisting.

Optimization to cut cost. Because the stress and deflection were so low, a beam with half the width was tested with the same forces and fixtures. The minimum factor of safety went up, not down, and the deflection rose to 0.00176 in, about the same as the top frame. The original side beams had their highest stresses in their curved sections, so the original beams were overbuilt and costing money for nothing.

8. Lessons Learned

Josef Chelala. I learned to calculate the reaction forces from the motors and leadscrews and to use them to pick brackets and set up an FEA. Small changes in geometry changed the stresses a lot: the top frame barely moved, but stress concentrations at the leadscrew holes pushed its factor of safety below 4. On the vertical frames, reducing the width raised the minimum factor of safety, which showed me that more material is not always stronger, and that I should find where and why stress occurs before adding material and cost.

Ethan King. I learned to select a motor from torque and speed requirements, to design a motor mount bracket, and to size a coupler with bearing and torsional stress calculations, and that alignment, strength, and sizing all matter in an assembly.
